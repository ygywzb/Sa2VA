\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

We evaluate Sa2VA-Select on image and video dense grounding, multimodal conversation, and efficiency profiling.
Unless otherwise specified, all comparisons are against Sa2VA-1B under multiple visual token retention ratios.

\subsection{Evaluation setup}
\paragraph{Retention ratios.}
We test retention ratios $\rho\in\{0.4,0.3,0.2,0.1\}$, corresponding to keeping the top 40\%/30\%/20\%/10\% visual tokens at inference.

\paragraph{Tasks and metrics.}
For image referring segmentation, we report cIoU on RefCOCO, RefCOCO+, and RefCOCOg.
For video referring segmentation, we report J\&F on MeViS, Ref-DAVIS17, ReVOS, and Ref-YTVOS; MeViS (val\_u) is also used in efficiency analysis.
For multimodal conversation, we follow standard benchmark protocols, including MME, MMBench, SEED-Bench, AI2D, MMStar, MMMU, and SQA$^{\mathrm{test}}$.

\paragraph{Training datasets and finetuning scope.}
Table~\ref{tab:train_datasets_seg} summarizes the segmentation-related data used for finetuning.
We initialize from a Sa2VA-pretrained checkpoint and finetune only LoRA parameters and the learnable attention parameters in LIS, while keeping the remaining backbone frozen.

\paragraph{Dataset sampling and repeats.}
We use a concatenated training dataset with repeat factors 5/5/4 for RefCOCO/RefCOCO+/RefCOCOg and 10/4/4/4 for ReVOS/MeViS/Ref-YTVOS/Ref-SAV.

\paragraph{Implementation details.}
Our backbone is InternVL2.5-1B initialized from the Sa2VA-1B checkpoint; the LLM and visual encoder are frozen.
We train LoRA with $r=128$, $\alpha=256$, dropout $0.05$, and a lightweight TransformerScorer (in\_features=896) for LIS.
The training budget is 0.3, while inference uses retention ratios 0.1/0.2/0.3/0.4.
We train for 1 epoch with per-device batch size 2 and gradient accumulation 4 on 2$\times$A100-SXM4-40GB GPUs.
Optimization uses AdamW with learning rate $4\times10^{-5}$, betas $(0.9,0.999)$, weight decay 0.05, and gradient clipping at 1.0.
We use bfloat16 AMP, 8 dataloader workers, 5\% linear warmup, and cosine annealing.
The tokenizer follows the \texttt{qwen\_chat} template with max length 8192 and special tokens [SEG], \texttt{<p>}, \texttt{</p>}, \texttt{<vp>}, \texttt{</vp>}.
Images are resized to target length 1024. Loss weights are 2.0 for mask loss and 0.5 for Dice loss.
For CAS, we use a linear schedule with $\lambda_{\mathrm{start}}=0.1$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{end}}=3.0$.

\subsection{Main results across tasks}
We report the main results in Table~\ref{tab:overall_table6_summary} and Table~\ref{tab:image_level_bench_sa2va1b_dev}.
Overall, learnable token selection remains effective after being transferred to Sa2VA.

\paragraph{Comparison with strong baselines.}
Table~\ref{tab:overall_table6_summary} presents a summary across image segmentation, video segmentation, and chat-oriented benchmarks.
Compared with Sa2VA-1B, Sa2VA-Select-1B at 40\% retention improves image referring segmentation on RefCOCOg from 72.3 to 74.8, video referring segmentation on MeViS from 41.7 to 44.9, ReVOS from 47.6 to 51.2, and Ref-YTVOS from 65.3 to 66.2, while remaining competitive on Ref-DAVIS17 (72.3 to 71.0).
The 30\% model gives the same 102\% relative average as the 40\% model and achieves the best Ref-YTVOS and Video-MME scores in the summary table.
Across these settings, the chat benchmarks remain close to the baseline.

\paragraph{Detailed results and retention sweep.}
Table~\ref{tab:image_level_bench_sa2va1b_dev} provides a more detailed retention sweep on image-level benchmarks.
Moderate pruning at 30--40\% retention largely preserves the baseline, whereas 10--20\% retention degrades general QA-oriented benchmarks more clearly.
RefCOCOg stays at or above the baseline across several retention ratios, so we treat 30--40\% as the preferred operating region.

\subsection{Qualitative analysis}
\label{sec:exp_qualitative}
We group representative video cases by recurring failure modes of the Sa2VA-1B baseline.
Across ReVOS and MeViS, the dominant pattern is distractor evidence in the full visual stream, especially when the target is small, partially visible, temporally delayed, or described by relational cues.

\paragraph{Distractor-dominant confusion.}
The first group of cases highlights failures caused by visually salient but semantically irrelevant objects.
In these examples, baseline predictions are pulled toward larger, more persistent, or more visually dominant regions.
Figure~\ref{fig:qual_distractor} makes this failure mode concrete.
In the LV-VIS case ``the yellow dog(s) on the left side of the frame'', the baseline drifts toward the dominant dog, whereas Sa2VA-Select keeps the referred left-side dog and improves J\&F from 23.8 to 98.2.
In the MOSE case ``Which object is a cat with a tail of black and white stripes but its body cannot be seen?'', the baseline is repeatedly attracted to the foreground kitten and reaches only 2.9 J\&F, while Sa2VA-Select reaches 90.5 J\&F.

\subsection{Ablation study}
\label{sec:exp_ablation}
All ablation runs use the same 30\% visual token budget.
We compare the plain Sa2VA-1B baseline, a selector without curriculum annealing, and several CAS schedules.

\paragraph{Effect of CAS and schedule strength.}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation_budget30} shows a clear trend.
Using LIS+DiffTopK alone already yields a workable selector, but the fixed-$\lambda_t$ variants remain below the baseline on most dense grounding metrics.
This suggests that a scorer and a budget constraint alone are not sufficient.
Adding CAS with an annealed schedule substantially improves performance, indicating that the selector benefits from first learning task-relevant token rankings under soft selection and then matching hard Top-$k$ behavior more strictly.
Among the tested schedules, $\lambda_t:0.1\!\sim\!3$ gives the best overall average (101.6\%) and the best ReVOS score (51.3), while remaining competitive on RefCOCO and DAVIS.
The milder $0.1\!\sim\!1$ schedule produces the best RefCOCOg score (74.7), while the best overall trade-off is obtained at $0.1\!\sim\!3$.

\subsection{Efficiency analysis}
We evaluate efficiency on MeViS (val\_u) on a single A100-SXM4-40GB GPU.
We report three metrics: maximum GPU memory, prefill time, and end-to-end (E2E) latency.
Prefill time is measured from the start of \texttt{generate} to the first full-sequence forward (sequence length $\neq 1$), while E2E latency measures the entire \texttt{generate} call, including both prefill and decoding.
Peak memory is the maximum allocated GPU memory during generation.

Table~\ref{tab:efficiency_mevis} summarizes the performance--efficiency trade-off across retention ratios, where ``Perf.'' denotes the average segmentation performance relative to Sa2VA-1B.
As expected, lower retention consistently reduces peak memory and prefill time.
The smallest memory footprint is obtained at 10\% retention (2.37~GB), and the fastest prefill is also obtained at 10\% (34.78~ms).
The best E2E latency is achieved at 20\% retention (204.96~ms), slightly below the Sa2VA-1B baseline (216.12~ms).
At the same time, 30--40\% retention preserves stronger dense grounding performance, especially on video benchmarks, but does not always minimize total latency.
This means the most accurate retention ratio is not necessarily the most efficient one.

\paragraph{Trade-off frontier.}
Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff_panels} makes the operating region more explicit.
In the performance panel, both the overall relative average and the ReVOS curve peak in the 30--40\% retention range.
In the efficiency panel, maximum GPU memory decreases steadily, while prefill time drops sharply from 40\% to 30\% and then stabilizes.
Together, the two panels identify 30--40\% retention as the preferred operating region.
